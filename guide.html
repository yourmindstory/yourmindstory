It’s Not Him.
It’s the Hook.

Why knowing better hasn’t stopped the pull.

If you’ve downloaded this, I’m guessing you’ve probably spent a lot of time searching for information about him.

Why did he change? Why did he pull away? Why did he come back? Is he avoidant? Did he love me? Does he miss me? Will he come back?

This, I’m hoping, will start being the end of your search for endless information about him.

Because this isn’t more information about him.

This is information about what happened to you.

How you got hooked. Why knowing better hasn’t stopped the pull. Why you can still be caught in it even if the relationship ended months ago.

And, more importantly, what you actually need to work on if you want your mind back.

01

I don’t really care what label he has.

Maybe you know he’s avoidant. Maybe you’ve watched so many videos about avoidant attachment that you understand exactly why he pulls away.

To be honest, I don’t really care about the label.

I care that the pattern of behaviour has you watching video after video trying to make sense of something that still doesn’t make sense to you.

And while you’re doing that, maybe you’re also promising yourself you won’t check again. You won’t look at whether he’s online. You won’t reread the messages. You won’t check his socials. You won’t message.

Then you do.

Or maybe you don’t do any of that anymore because this relationship ended a while ago.

But he is still in your head.

You still go back to the beginning. You still think about what changed. You still wonder what you missed or what you could have done differently. Maybe some part of you is still hoping the version of him you met at the beginning is the real one and somehow that version is going to come back.

That is the part I’m interested in.

Not another explanation of him.

Why is this still happening to you?
02

So how did you get hooked?

Probably without realising it was happening.

Things were good, then they weren’t.

He was close, then distant.

Warm, then cold.

Maybe something would go wrong and you’d think, right, I can’t keep doing this.

Then he would message.

Or you’d have a really good weekend.

He would suddenly be affectionate again.

You’d finally have the conversation you’d been waiting for and think, okay, maybe we’re alright now.

And for a while, you felt better.

Then it changed again.

So every time you started adjusting to the distance, there was just enough of the good stuff to bring the hope back.

And if it’s over now, it doesn’t necessarily mean that part disappeared when he did.

You can still remember how it felt at the beginning. You can still remember who he was when things were good. You can still think about the future you thought you were going to have.

Sometimes you’re not waiting for the man anymore.

You’re waiting for what you thought it was going to become.
03

This is where intermittent reinforcement comes in.

There is a well-established effect in behavioural psychology around what happens when rewards are inconsistent.

In plain English, you don’t have to get the thing you want every time to keep going back for it.

Actually, when you don’t know when you’re going to get it, the behaviour can become very persistent. That’s what researchers mean when they talk about partial or intermittent reinforcement.

Think about what was happening in the relationship.

Sometimes you reached for him and got warmth. Sometimes you got nothing. Sometimes he reassured you. Sometimes he disappeared. Sometimes you thought it was over, then suddenly you had him back.

So your mind learned something very simple:

Maybe this time.

Maybe this time he’ll reply. Maybe this time it will last. Maybe this time we’ll have the conversation. Maybe this time I’ll get the person from the beginning back.

And this is the bit I think is really important.

The reward wasn’t always just him.

Sometimes the reward was the relief.

You’ve been anxious for hours. Your stomach is in knots. You’re checking your phone and trying not to message him.

Then his name appears.

And what happens?

For a minute, you’re okay again.

That matters.

Because now contact from him isn’t only something you want. It has also become something that can take away the horrible feeling you were having before he contacted you.

Then the cycle starts again.

04

This is why I keep making the comparison with your phone.

I don’t mean your relationship was literally an app.

I mean the reason you keep checking your phone is actually very easy to understand.

Something good does not happen every single time you pick it up.

Most of the time, nothing particularly interesting happens.

But every now and then there is something you wanted to see.

So later, you check again.

You don’t keep checking because every check gives you something.

You keep checking because one of them might.

That is the parallel.

You didn’t know which message was going to be the warm one. Which silence was going to end. Which argument was going to be followed by affection. Which time he came back might finally be the time he stayed.

And interestingly, this type of unpredictable reward is one of the mechanisms specifically raised in the major US litigation around addictive social-media design. The court records describe “intermittent variable rewards”, including strategically timing rewards and notifications to keep people engaged and checking.

In March 2026, a Los Angeles jury also found Meta and Google negligent in the design and operation of Instagram and YouTube in a landmark case concerning harmful compulsive use.

I’m not telling you this because I want you to learn another theory.

I’m telling you because I want you to see that the unpredictability matters.

It helps explain how you can receive so little from someone and still spend so much time thinking about them.

There is even relationship research where women who were uncertain about how much potential partners liked them thought about those men more, and that increased thinking was associated with greater attraction.

So the inconsistency was not some irrelevant part of what happened.

It was part of what kept you looking for the next good moment.

05

And no, he doesn’t have to still be there.

This is important because you could read everything I’ve just written and think:

But we aren’t even together anymore. He isn’t doing any of that now.

That doesn’t mean you’re not still dealing with what happened.

You can have him blocked and still think about him every day.

You can know you wouldn’t actually take him back and still check whether he’s with someone else.

You can be getting on with your life and then hear one song and suddenly you’re right back in something that hasn’t existed for six months.

You might not check at all anymore. You might be doing everything you think you’re supposed to be doing.

But he’s still taking up far more space in your head than you want him to.

And sometimes what you’re waiting for now isn’t even contact.

You’re waiting for an apology. An explanation. For him to finally understand what he did. For the whole thing to make sense.

So he doesn’t have to still be actively doing anything for you to still be dealing with what happened.

06

Here’s where I think women can get stuck again.

You finally understand what happened.

You learn about avoidant attachment. Then intermittent reinforcement. Then nervous-system regulation. Trauma bonding. No contact.

Whatever finally makes you think: Ohhhh. That’s what happened to me.

And that understanding can be incredibly relieving.

But then tomorrow comes.

And you still want to check. You still wake up thinking about him. Your body still reacts. You still struggle to stick to the decision you made last night.

So now I want you to notice something.

You could stop searching for information about him and start searching for endless information about yourself instead.

And still stay stuck.

Because eventually, more information becomes more information.

You can understand intermittent reinforcement perfectly and still be experiencing it. You can understand why your nervous system is reacting and still have the reaction. You can know exactly why you shouldn’t message him and still desperately want to.

So yes, I want you to understand what happened to you.

But I don’t want you to spend the next two years just becoming an expert in that instead.

Because if information alone was enough, you would already be out of this.

At some point, you have to work on what the pattern has actually done.
